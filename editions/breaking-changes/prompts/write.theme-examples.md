### What a good theme looks like

> A field study of teams that adopted AI code review finds that reviewers approve faster but leave fewer comments on design, and a post-incident writeup traces an outage to an unreviewed AI-suggested dependency. Two more pieces look at how teams are changing who owns what the assistant wrote.

This works because the lede *frames* the study (what it looked at, who) before quoting its finding, so a cold reader understands what is interesting without knowing the paper's name. The territory sentence gestures at the rest of the day by kind of practice, not by more tool names.

### What a bad theme looks like (dense list)

> Study A measures review latency, incident B is a typosquatted package, post C argues for pairing, release D adds a hooks API, and paper E benchmarks test generation at 41%.

This is a catalog, not editorial. A cold reader has no idea what any of these are or why they sit together.

### What a bad theme looks like (abstract-shaped)

> We find that AI-assisted developers complete tasks 26% faster across three companies, extending prior productivity work by a larger sample.

Reads like the paper's own abstract. A cold reader can't tell what was studied or what a team should take from it.

### What a bad theme looks like (generic)

> Today's items explore how AI is transforming software development, with new tools, practices and challenges for engineering teams navigating this shift.

A category label dressed as prose. Would describe 60% of every issue.
